The proposition

“The world is losing its forests.”

Source: IMF Climate Change Indicators (FAO forest indicators), 1992–2023. The charts below use forest area and (on the opposing side) forest carbon stocks.

1 | In support

Forest area has decreased, and some countries lost much more than others.

Two complementary views show the worldwide trend and the different scales of national forest loss.

Global forest area has fallen since 1992

A global total frames the change in absolute terms, showing the amount of forest lost.

Design Rationale
  • Truncated y-axis (−2): Global forest area decreased by only 4.2% between 1992 and 2023. Set the Y-axis start from 4,000 million hectares instead of starting at 0 to persuade readers that forest loss is serious. This makes the declining trend appear steeper.
  • Red Line and Shaded Area (−0.5): Used red to emphasize forest loss because the color is often associated with danger. The shaded area also makes the downward trend more noticeable.
  • Full Time Range (+1.5): Included all years from 1992 to 2023 instead of selecting only the years with the largest declines. This allows readers to see the long-term trend and provides more complete information.

Smaller countries can lose a larger share of forests

Line charts show forest area changes over time; bars compare percentage losses with absolute values labeled.

Design Rationale
  • Selected countries (−1): Selected countries with the three largest absolute forest losses and the three highest percentage losses because we wanted to compare countries of different sizes. However, this selection only shows countries losing forests and excludes countries with forest gains.
  • Indexing 1992 to 100 (+1.5): Used 1992 as the baseline (1992 = 100) because countries have different initial forest areas. This helps readers compare how much forest each country has lost relative to its original forest area.
  • Line + Bar Charts (+1.5): Shows how each country's forest changed over time and how much it lost.
  • Natural sequential reds (−0.5): Used a red gradient, with darker red representing a higher percentage of forest loss. This helps readers quickly notice countries with greater relative losses. Choosing red because it can create a stronger sense of environmental danger.
  • Percentage and hectare labels (+2): Added both percentage and absolute forest loss values because showing only one measurement could be misleading. The percentages help readers compare countries of different sizes, while the absolute values show how much forest was actually lost.
2 | In opposition

Forests are holding steady, and major countries are regrowing them.

Two views measure forests by the carbon they store and by where forest cover is expanding.

Forests still hold almost all of their carbon

Carbon stocks are indexed to 1992 so the world and both economic groups can be compared on one scale.

Design decisions and rationale
  • Measuring carbon instead of forest area (−1.5): Carbon stocks fell only 1.1% while forest area fell 4.2%, so switching metrics makes the same forests look stable. The subtitle names the indicator, but it never says why we chose it over area, which the proposition is really about.
  • Full 0–120 y-axis (−1): Starting at zero is the "honest" convention, which makes the chart look trustworthy, but here it flattens a loss of 3.3 billion tonnes of carbon into a nearly straight line. A zoomed axis like Figure 1's would show a clear decline.
  • Indexing to 1992 = 100 (−1): Indexing lets groups of very different sizes share an axis, but it hides that Advanced Economies hold only about 20% of forest carbon. Their rising line looks as important as the decline among Emerging and Developing Economies, which hold the other 80%.
  • Title and annotation framing (−0.5): "Still hold 99%" is accurate, but it presents the loss as what remains rather than what was lost. The title's "Advanced Economies are adding more" puts the one rising line first.
  • Final values in the legend (+1.5): Printing each group's 2023 index value (99, 111, 97) lets readers check the exact numbers without estimating them from the lines.

The biggest forest gains are larger than whole countries

A ranked bar chart shows the ten countries that added the most forest area.

Design decisions and rationale
  • Showing only gainers (−2): Ranking the top ten gainers and leaving out everyone else hides that 102 countries lost forest while 88 gained. Brazil alone lost 88.4M hectares, more than China gained. This filtering is the chart's main deception.
  • Absolute hectares instead of percentages (−1): Measuring change in hectares favors large countries, so China, India, the US and Russia lead the ranking. In percentage terms, the biggest changes are losses in smaller countries, as Figure 2 shows.
  • "Biggest economies" in the title (−1): The headline groups the chart under economic success, but Vietnam and Chile are not among the world's largest economies. Readers take in the title before they check the bar labels.
  • China vs. France comparison (−0.5): The comparison is accurate (64.5M ha gained vs. 53.9M ha of French land area) and makes an abstract number concrete. It also leaves out that FAO counts planted forests and plantations as forest, and a large share of China's gain comes from them.
  • Zero baseline and direct value labels (+1.5): Bar lengths are proportional, and each bar is labeled, so readers can compare the gains accurately.
